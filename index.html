Earthbound AI: Rivers and Pipelines

My first job was as a GIS engineer at Earthbound AI, a startup in California.

Pipelines in the US cross rivers thousands of times, usually a few metres under the bed, under wide valley rivers and creeks you could step across alike. Every one of those crossings rests on a simple assumption, that the river will stay where the map says it is. Rivers rarely cooperate. National river lines can be off by as much as 200 metres, a flood can scour away the ground over a pipe in a single night, and some channels drift sideways for years before anyone notices. At Earthbound I was the primary developer on three apps that help pipeline engineers stay ahead of all three problems.

App 1 of 3

Hydrotechnical dashboard

The first problem is the flood. The Hydrotechnical Dashboard models every crossing at an ordinary flow, a two- year flood and a ten-year flood, and works out how deep the fast water would dig into the bed and how much ground would be left over the pipe at each. The distinction that matters is between a crossing that floods and a crossing that loses its cover, because only the second puts the pipe at risk. By ranking crossings on that difference, the dashboard lets engineers send crews to the few that matter before a storm, instead of checking every crossing after one.

App 2 of 3

3D pipeline viewer

Despite all that modelling, a number on a dashboard is still an estimate of something buried. The 3D Pipeline Viewer closes that gap. It rebuilds the terrain around a pipe in three dimensions, places it on the map in its true position, and checks the modelled depth against what crews actually measured in the field. When the model and the ground disagree, an engineer finds out at a desk, which is a far cheaper place to be wrong than halfway through a dig.

App 3 of 3

Channel migration

The slowest problem is also the hardest to see. A river that drifts a few metres a year looks the same in any single photo, so Channel Migration reads years of aerial and satellite imagery at once, tracing each river with a model trained partly on outlines drawn by hand. It sorts every stretch of pipe near water into a crossing, a parallel run or an encroachment, and estimates how fast each bend is closing in. The angle turns out to matter as much as the speed. A river meeting a pipe head-on exposes a short span, while one running alongside can expose a long run all at once. Together, these let engineers deal with a moving bend years before a bank gives way.

Adgile Media: Trucks, Pings and Proof

Later I took a part-time role at Adgile Media Group to build their GPS fleet monitoring system.

Adgile puts advertising on the sides of trucks and sends them through New York, Los Angeles and other US cities. Unlike a fixed billboard, the audience changes with every turn, so every campaign comes down to one question, whether the right people actually saw it. The Fleet Dashboard I built answers that for a fleet of more than 500 trucks, turning a constant stream of GPS readings into evidence a campaign can be sold on.

Step 1 of 3

From pings to streets

That evidence starts with messy data. Every truck reports its position every few seconds, but raw GPS drifts and now and then jumps a whole block, which would quietly inflate any number built on it. The platform matches each trace to the streets the truck actually drove and gives every route a confidence score, so the weak ones are flagged and left out instead of counted.

Step 2 of 3

Who could have seen it

Even a clean route says little on its own. A mile down a busy shopping street and a mile past empty warehouses are not worth the same, so the platform joins each stretch of route with daily audience data and measures a campaign in people reached, not ground covered.

Step 3 of 3

Proof for the campaign

Most questions about a campaign are local. When Adgile wants to know how one did in a particular neighbourhood, they draw that neighbourhood on the map as a geofence. The platform clips every truck's route to it and returns the miles, the minutes and the impressions inside. That report is what a campaign is judged on, sold on and renewed on.

Now: NYU CUSP and the CERA Lab

This fall I moved to Brooklyn, and from building tools to asking questions with them.

I'm doing a master's in Urban Data Science at NYU's Center for Urban Science + Progress. I'm also a graduate research assistant in the CERA Lab, working with Professors Augustin Guibaud and Yuki Miura on how cities adapt to wildfire. We're comparing sites in Los Angeles and Tulsa, lining up satellite imagery with land-management records, and mapping what was destroyed and rebuilt before and after a fire.
